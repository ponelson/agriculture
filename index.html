A farm is a factory with the roof off

Everything peculiar about agriculture follows from that. Production runs once a year and cannot be changed once the seed is in the ground; output is decided by weather after the money is spent; the benchmark price is set in Chicago by the whole world's harvest; and the inputs are sold by a handful of companies whose own costs are set by natural gas and shipping lanes. This guide starts at zero and follows the crop from the soil through the elevator, the barge, the trading house and the futures pit to the plate — in a year in which a war in the Gulf doubled the price of fertiliser and the grain price did not follow.

01 / Orientation

What a farm business actually is

A factory with the roof off: one production run a year, an output price set in Chicago, input prices set in a few boardrooms and a gas field, and the weather as an uninvited partner.

A corn farmer in Iowa makes most of the year's important decisions in a few weeks of spring — what to plant, how much nitrogen to apply, how much of the crop to sell before it exists — and then waits five months to learn whether they were right. Production cannot be adjusted once the seed is in the ground. Output is uncertain until the combine runs. The price received is set by a futures market that reflects the whole world's supply, of which any single farm is an invisible fraction. And the farmer is a price-taker on both sides: selling into a market dominated by a handful of grain traders and processors, buying from a handful of seed, chemical, fertiliser and equipment companies.

Where the opening line breaks

The opening is a heuristic for annual row crops, and it bends in three places. Price: Chicago futures are a benchmark, but farmers sell at a local cash price equal to futures plus basis, which can move by tens of cents a bushel with harvest pressure, freight and local demand, and by more in disrupted years; rice, palm oil, several wheat classes and most specialty crops price against other exchanges or none at all. Production: orchards, vineyards, livestock, dairy and greenhouse growers do not run one season a year with the roof off. Timing: Southern Hemisphere harvests and double-cropping give world grain markets more than one harvest a year.

That produces the organising fact of the industry. Agriculture is a biological, once-a-year, irreversible bet, hedged with financial instruments built for exactly that problem. Futures markets were invented in nineteenth-century Chicago so that farmers, elevators and millers could transfer price risk to people willing to bear it. Crop insurance, built on those futures prices, transfers yield and revenue risk to the government and insurers. The rest of the supply chain — elevators, railroads, barges, export terminals, crushers, ethanol plants, packers — is an industrial system designed to move a harvest that arrives all at once into a demand that lasts all year.

The second organising fact is that governments are permanent participants. They subsidise crop insurance, write minimum revenue guarantees into farm bills, mandate biofuel use that consumes a third of the US corn crop, negotiate purchase commitments with foreign governments, and write emergency cheques when trade wars or price collapses threaten farm incomes. No major agricultural market anywhere is free of policy.

The spine of the supply chain

Land → seed and genetics → fertiliser and crop protection → planting → weather → harvest → on-farm storage → country elevator → rail, barge or truck → export terminal or processor (crusher, ethanol plant, mill, feedlot, packer) → food manufacturer → retailer → plate  ·  and above it all, the futures price that every cash price is quoted against

Two links carry most of the economics. Fertiliser, because nitrogen is made from natural gas and much of the world's traded supply moves through the Strait of Hormuz — which is why a war in the Gulf has been the dominant cost story on Midwestern farms in 2026. And the futures price, because every cash price on every farm and elevator in America is quoted as a basis — a premium or discount — to a contract trading on the Chicago Board of Trade.

Four things that make agriculture unlike other industries

1

One production run a year

A factory can change its output next week. A farm decides once, in spring, and lives with it. Supply responds to price with a lag of a full season — in tree crops like coffee and cocoa, several years.

2

The weather sets output

A single hot, dry fortnight in July, during corn pollination, can move national yields by several percent. No other large industry has its output determined by an uncontrollable input after the capital is committed.

3

The hourglass

Millions of farmers at the bottom, billions of eaters at the top, and a narrow waist of a few traders, processors, packers and input suppliers in the middle. Bargaining power, and much of the margin, sits at the waist.

4

Policy is part of the price

Biofuel mandates, insurance subsidies, reference prices, export restrictions and foreign purchase commitments are not background — they are demand and supply in their own right.

Livestock is grain on legs. A large share of the world's corn and soybean meal is fed to animals, and the conversion rates matter: broadly, it takes around two pounds of feed to produce a pound of chicken, three to four for pork and six or more for beef in a feedlot. Changes in meat demand therefore ripple straight back into grain. In the US, the cattle herd fell to its smallest in decades by 2025 after years of drought and high feed costs, pushing cattle prices to records; rebuilding a herd takes years, because heifers kept back for breeding are heifers not sold for beef.

The United States plants roughly 175–180 million acres to corn and soybeans combined each year, overwhelmingly in rotation across the Corn Belt. The rotation is agronomic — soybeans fix nitrogen that the following corn crop uses, and rotation breaks pest cycles — but the acreage split each spring is an economic decision driven by the ratio of new-crop soybean to corn prices and by the cost of nitrogen, which corn needs and soybeans largely do not.

03 / The farm business

Revenue is a product of two things you do not control

Price times yield, minus costs that are mostly fixed by planting time. The farm's margin lives in a narrow band that weather and Chicago can each erase.

American farms are overwhelmingly family-owned, and most cropland is farmed by a relatively small number of large commercial operations; around two-fifths of US farmland is rented rather than owned by the person farming it. Rent is either cash rent — a fixed amount per acre, so the tenant bears all price and yield risk — or a crop share, in which landlord and tenant split the crop and some costs. Cash rents adjust to land values and recent profitability with a lag, which is one reason farm margins compress for years after a boom: rents set in good years outlast the good years.

The per-acre budget below is the most important document on a grain farm. It shows how thin the margin is. At typical 2026 Corn Belt numbers, a corn crop on rented ground costs on the order of $850–950 an acre to grow; a 195-bushel crop sold at about $4.50 cash brings in about $880. A ten-bushel yield miss or a thirty-cent price move is the whole margin.

That is why farmers reach for the only remaining levers — marketing the crop well, and insurance — and why government payments, when they come, can be the difference between profit and loss for a whole region.

04 / Inputs

The suppliers with pricing power

Four sets of companies supply what a farm needs to plant, and each is far more concentrated than the farms it sells to.

Seed

Genetics and traits

Corn and soybean seed is sold as proprietary hybrids and varieties carrying licensed biotech traits for insect and herbicide resistance. Bayer (which bought Monsanto in 2018), Corteva (which has announced plans to separate its seed and crop-protection businesses), Syngenta Group (owned by Chinese state company Sinochem) and BASF dominate globally. Seed is the input farmers are least able to substitute.

Fertiliser

Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium

Nitrogen is made from natural gas through the Haber-Bosch process — gas is 70–90% of the cost of ammonia — so nitrogen prices follow gas prices and gas geography: Russia, the Gulf, Trinidad, the US. Phosphate comes from rock concentrated in Morocco, China, the US and Russia. Potash is mined mainly in Canada, Russia and Belarus. Each is an oligopoly.

Chemicals

Crop protection

Herbicides, insecticides and fungicides, from the same few multinationals plus a large off-patent generic industry, much of it manufactured in China and India. Glyphosate resistance in weeds has driven up herbicide use and cost.

Machinery

Equipment and data

Deere dominates large North American row-crop equipment, followed by CNH, AGCO and Kubota. A new high-capacity combine costs over $1 million. Precision agriculture — GPS guidance, variable-rate application, yield mapping — has made equipment a data platform, and right-to-repair disputes over that software are now a policy issue.

Why fertiliser is the geopolitical input. Nitrogen fertiliser is, in effect, natural gas in solid form, and it is cheapest to make where gas is cheapest and nobody else wants it — Russia, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Egypt, Trinidad. Three of the world's ten largest urea exporters ship through the Strait of Hormuz. China, also a major producer, periodically restricts exports to protect domestic prices. So when a war closes a Gulf shipping lane, or China closes its export window, or Europe's gas price spikes, the cost of growing corn in Illinois moves within days.

The industry tracks this with simple ratios: the corn–urea ratio — how many bushels of corn buy a ton of urea — and its soybean equivalent. When the ratio is high, farmers trim nitrogen rates, shift acres toward soybeans, or both. In early March 2026, before the worst of the price spike, the corn–urea ratio was already near 87–90 bushels per ton, a five-year high, according to CME Group's market commentary.

The first ten inches. Most of what makes a field productive is soil: organic matter, drainage, water-holding capacity, pH. Tile drainage installed decades ago is why much of the former wet prairie of Iowa, Illinois and Indiana is now among the most productive cropland on earth. Soil quality is capitalised into land values and rents — the Corn Suitability Rating in Iowa and productivity indices in Illinois are used directly in land sales and appraisals.
05 / Weather, yield and the balance sheet

How the world decides what grain is worth

Prices are set by a single number that the whole market is trying to forecast: how much will be left over at the end of the year.

Every major crop has a supply and demand balance sheet, published monthly by the US Department of Agriculture in the World Agricultural Supply and Demand Estimates (WASDE) and debated by every analyst in the trade. The arithmetic is simple. Beginning stocks plus production plus imports gives total supply. Feed, food, industrial use (ethanol, crush), and exports give total use. What remains is ending stocks, and the ratio of ending stocks to use — stocks-to-use — is the single most important determinant of price.

The relationship is non-linear, and that is the key insight. When stocks are ample, a 100-million-bushel change in the corn crop barely moves the price, because there is plenty in the bins either way. When stocks are tight, the same change can move price by a dollar or more, because the market must ration demand — price must rise until some ethanol plant, feedlot or importer stops buying. This is why grain prices sit quietly for years and then double in a drought summer, as in 2012.

Ending stocks = beginning stocks + production + imports − (feed + food/industrial + exports)

South America has made the calendar global. Brazil now grows two corn crops a year — the second, safrinha, planted straight after the soybean harvest — and is the world's largest soybean producer and exporter by a wide margin. Its soybean harvest from January to April competes directly with US exports in the months when US old-crop supplies are being drawn down, and Argentina's crop arrives soon after. Weather in Mato Grosso in January now matters to Iowa farmers almost as much as weather in Iowa in July.

Trend yield. US corn yields have risen about two bushels per acre per year for decades, from genetics, agronomy, fertiliser and equipment. Analysts measure each season against that trend, which in recent years has run in the low 180s. Yield is the variable a forecaster most often gets wrong; acreage is the one the market most often underestimates.
06 / Storage, logistics and basis

Moving a harvest that arrives all at once

The crop comes in over eight weeks and is eaten over fifty-two. The industry's physical infrastructure exists to store it, move it and price the difference.

At harvest, grain goes either to bins on the farm or to a country elevator, where it is weighed, graded, dried if necessary and stored or shipped. US grading standards (No. 2 yellow corn, No. 1 yellow soybeans) let grain from thousands of farms be pooled and traded as a commodity, with premiums and discounts for moisture, damage and foreign material. That standardisation, pioneered at the Chicago Board of Trade in the 1850s, is what made a futures market possible: you cannot trade a contract on a bushel unless a bushel means the same thing everywhere.

From elevators, grain moves by truck to processors, by unit and shuttle trains of 100 or more cars on BNSF and Union Pacific to the Pacific Northwest and the Gulf, and by barge down the Illinois and Mississippi rivers to export elevators around New Orleans. The Gulf typically handles the majority of US corn and soybean exports and the PNW much of the rest. River levels matter: low water on the Mississippi in the autumns of 2022–24 forced barges to carry lighter loads, sent barge freight rates to records and widened basis across the interior.

Basis: the price of place and time

Every cash grain price in America is quoted as basis — the difference between the local cash price and the nearby futures price. A basis of "35 under December" means the elevator is paying 35 cents below the December corn futures price. Basis reflects the cost of transport from that location to where the grain is demanded, local supply and demand, storage availability and quality. It is typically weakest (most negative) at harvest, when every farmer is delivering at once and storage is full, and strengthens through the year as supplies are drawn down.

Basis is why the futures price is not the farmer's price. A farmer who hedges with futures has eliminated the risk of a falling flat price but kept basis risk — the risk that the local cash price moves differently from Chicago. For most farmers basis is more predictable than futures, which is why hedging works; but in a drought, a river closure or a local plant shutdown, basis can move as much as futures.

Cash price = futures price + basis   ·   Basis = cash − futures
1

Carry

When later futures trade above nearby futures, the market is paying to store grain. A carry close to the full cost of storage and interest tells farmers and elevators to hold the crop.

2

Inverse

When nearby futures trade above deferred — an inverted market — the market wants grain now. Storage loses money; sell.

3

Convergence

At delivery, futures and cash at delivery points must converge, or arbitrageurs would deliver against the futures. Convergence is what ties Chicago to the physical world.

4

Spreads

Old-crop/new-crop spreads (July/December corn, July/November soybeans) express the market's view of how tight supplies will be before the new harvest arrives.

07 / Traders and processors

The narrow waist of the hourglass

A handful of trading houses originate, store, ship and process most of the world's traded grain and oilseeds. Their business is margins, logistics and information, not price direction.

The great grain traders are known as the ABCD: Archer-Daniels-Midland, Bunge (which merged with Viterra in 2025), Cargill and Louis Dreyfus — joined by China's state-owned COFCO International and Asian players such as Wilmar and Olam. Dan Morgan's 1979 book Merchants of Grain described them as the most secretive large companies in the world; Cargill and Louis Dreyfus remain privately controlled. They own country elevators, river terminals, export elevators, ships, crushing plants, flour mills and ethanol plants across the Americas, Europe and Asia.

Their business is margins: buying grain at one place and time and form and selling it at another. Origination margin (buying from farmers below what it can be resold for), freight and logistics margin, storage carry, quality blending, and processing margin. They hedge flat-price exposure in futures almost entirely and make money from basis, spreads and processing — and from knowing, through their global origination and shipping networks, where supply and demand are shifting before anyone else does.

Biofuel policy is the biggest demand story in US farming. The Renewable Fuel Standard requires refiners to blend set volumes of renewable fuels, tracked through tradeable credits called RINs. 08 / Futures markets

A contract for a bushel that does not exist yet

Grain futures were invented to solve a farmer's problem. They now set the price of almost every bushel traded in the world.

A futures contract is a standardised, exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell a fixed quantity of a specified grade of a commodity, at a specified delivery location and month, at a price agreed today. Standardisation is the point: because every CBOT corn contract is 5,000 bushels of No. 2 yellow corn deliverable in the same way, contracts are interchangeable, and anyone can trade them anonymously against anyone else, with the exchange's clearing house standing between every buyer and seller and guaranteeing performance.

The guarantee works through margin. Both sides post an initial margin — a performance bond, typically a small percentage of the contract's value — and every day the clearing house marks to market: losses are collected from one side and paid to the other in cash. A farmer who is short futures as a hedge will receive cash when prices fall and must pay cash when they rise, even though the physical crop in the bin has risen in value by a similar amount. This daily cash flow is the hidden cost of hedging and the reason a grain elevator's banker is as important as its trader.

Very few futures end in delivery — most are closed out before the delivery period — but delivery is what makes the price real. For CBOT corn and soybeans, delivery is by shipping certificates issued by approved warehouses along the Illinois River; the possibility that someone will deliver, or take delivery, forces futures and cash to converge as expiry approaches.

Prices on the CBOT are quoted in cents and quarter-cents per bushel: "four eighty-three and a quarter" is $4.8325. Daily price limits, reset twice a year, cap how far grain futures can move in a session. Options on futures give the right but not the obligation to buy (calls) or sell (puts) at a strike price, in exchange for a premium; implied volatility in grain options rises reliably into the summer weather market and collapses after it.

Who is on the other side of the farmer? Commercials — elevators, processors, exporters, food companies — hedge their physical positions. Speculators, including managed-money funds, provide liquidity and absorb risk in exchange for expected return. The CFTC's weekly Commitments of Traders report shows how each group is positioned; when funds hold a record short position, a weather scare can produce a violent short-covering rally.

History in one paragraph. The Chicago Board of Trade was founded in 1848. Merchants traded "to arrive" forward contracts through the 1850s, and the Board introduced standardised futures with margin requirements in 1865. William Cronon's Nature's Metropolis shows how grading, elevators, railroads and the telegraph turned grain from a farmer's sack into an abstract commodity. Onions remain the only farm product whose futures trading is banned by federal law, after a 1950s cornering scandal.
09 / Hedging and marketing

How farmers and merchants manage price

Most farmers never touch a futures account. They hedge through the elevator, which hedges in Chicago. The tools differ in who bears which risk.

The simplest marketing plan is to harvest, deliver to the elevator and take the cash price that day — fully exposed to price, and typically at the year's weakest basis. Everything else is a way of fixing part of the price earlier, or later, or with a floor.

Futures hedge lifted at harvest; put held to expiry and exercised or abandoned at its intrinsic value; forward-contract bushels short of the commitment are bought back at the harvest cash price. No commissions, interest on margin, or storage. Real marketing plans scale in over months, mix tools, and are sized to insured rather than expected bushels.

The dangerous combination. Set the harvest futures price high and the yield low — a drought year, which is exactly when both happen together. The short-futures hedger and the forward-contracted farmer have committed bushels they did not grow, and must buy back the shortfall at the high price. This is natural hedge logic: for a large producing region, low yields cause high prices, so an unhedged farmer's revenue is partly self-stabilising. It is why marketing advisers size hedges to bushels covered by revenue insurance rather than expected bushels, and why puts — which cost a premium but carry no delivery obligation — are popular despite the cost.

Elevators carry the liquidity risk. When a farmer signs a forward contract, the elevator sells futures to hedge it. If prices rise sharply before harvest, the elevator faces large margin calls on those short futures while the offsetting gain sits in contracts with farmers that will not pay cash until delivery. In 2008, 2012 and 2022, rising prices left some grain companies scrambling for credit, and a handful failed. A grain merchant's balance sheet is a liquidity machine as much as a trading book.

10 / Government and insurance

The state as risk-bearer of last resort

US farm policy now runs mainly through subsidised insurance tied to futures prices, statutory reference prices, biofuel mandates and — increasingly — emergency payments.

The federal crop insurance programme is the centre of US farm support. Policies are sold by private insurers, reinsured by the government, and subsidised on average at around 60% of premium. The most popular product, Revenue Protection, guarantees a share of expected revenue calculated from the farm's own yield history and the average futures price in February. If the October harvest futures price is higher, the guarantee rises to it — which is what lets a farmer forward-sell or hedge bushels without fear of being unable to deliver them in a drought. Insurance and hedging are designed to work together.

Revenue to count = actual yield × harvest price. Real policies use actual production history adjustments, quality adjustments, replant provisions and price limits on the harvest price. Premium is the farmer's share after federal subsidy, which is larger at lower coverage levels and for enterprise units.

The farm bill and the 2025 reset

Alongside insurance, commodity programmes pay when prices or county revenues fall below statutory levels. Price Loss Coverage (PLC) pays when the national season-average price falls below an effective reference price; Agriculture Risk Coverage (ARC) pays when county revenue falls below a benchmark. The 2025 budget law rewrote both, raising statutory reference prices roughly 10–20% — corn to $4.10 a bushel, soybeans to $10.00 and wheat to $6.35 — increasing premium subsidies and the supplemental coverage option, and raising payment limits. With USDA projecting a $4.70 season-average corn price, PLC is out of the money for corn this year; it was designed for the next price collapse.

On top of permanent programmes sit ad hoc payments, which have become routine. The 2018–19 trade war produced about $23 billion of Market Facilitation Program payments; pandemic relief followed; the 2025 Emergency Commodity Assistance Program paid about $10 billion; and a $12 billion bridge-assistance package in December 2025 compensated farmers for the 2025 trade disruption with China. Farmers increasingly plan around the expectation that Washington will act when margins collapse — which itself influences planting and land prices.

Trade

Purchase commitments

China halted most US soybean purchases in 2025 during the tariff dispute, then agreed in the November 2025 Busan arrangement to buy 25 million tonnes a year through 2028 — while keeping its 10% retaliatory tariff, so purchases run through state buyers meeting a political target rather than commercial demand.

Trade

Export restrictions

Exporters restrict sales when domestic prices rise: India's rice and wheat bans, Russia's wheat export quotas and taxes, Indonesia's 2022 palm oil ban. Each moves world prices more than the volumes suggest, because only a small share of production is traded.

Energy

Biofuel mandates

The Renewable Fuel Standard, state low-carbon fuel standards and federal tax credits create demand for corn and vegetable oils that would not otherwise exist — and make farm incomes sensitive to EPA rulemakings and court decisions on refinery exemptions.

Abroad

Other systems

The EU's Common Agricultural Policy pays largely by area, with environmental conditions; China holds large state reserves and supports domestic prices; Brazil relies on subsidised credit rather than insurance.

11 / Land, finance and tax

The farm's real balance sheet

Farmland is most farmers' largest asset, most lenders' main collateral, and the main channel through which farm profits are capitalised and passed between generations.

US farm real estate averaged around $4,350 an acre and cropland around $5,830 in USDA's 2025 survey, with the best Iowa and Illinois ground selling for several times that. Cash rents typically yield the landowner only 2–3% of land value, so most of a landowner's return comes from appreciation — which has historically been steady, inflation-linked and occasionally catastrophic. The 1980s farm crisis is the industry's defining memory: after a decade of rising prices and cheap credit, interest rates above 15% and collapsing export demand cut Midwestern land values by around half, and thousands of farms and hundreds of rural banks failed.

Most farmland is bought by neighbouring farmers. Institutional ownership — insurers, pension funds such as TIAA/Nuveen, public REITs such as Farmland Partners and Gladstone Land, and wealthy individuals, of whom Bill Gates is the most famous — is a small share of the total but a prominent political issue, as is foreign ownership, which several states now restrict. Farm debt exceeds half a trillion dollars, lent principally by the cooperative Farm Credit System and by commercial and community banks, with USDA's Farm Service Agency as lender of last resort for beginning and stressed farmers.

Tax: a code written for farms

1

Cash accounting and prepaying

Most farms may use cash-method accounting, deducting inputs when paid and recognising income when crops are sold. Buying next year's fertiliser and seed in December, or deferring crop sales into January, smooths taxable income across good and bad years — a routine year-end exercise on every grain farm.

2

Income averaging

Farmers can spread a high-income year over the prior three years' brackets on Schedule J, softening the progressive-rate penalty of volatile income.

3

Equipment write-offs

Section 179 expensing and bonus depreciation — restored to 100% permanently by the 2025 budget law — let farms deduct machinery purchases immediately, which drives equipment sales in profitable years and is one reason dealers' order books track farm income so closely.

4

Land and estates

Section 1031 like-kind exchanges let landowners trade farmland without recognising gain; special-use valuation under Section 2032A values farmland at its agricultural rather than market value for estate tax; and the federal estate exemption rose to $15 million per person from 2026. Conservation easements can yield large charitable deductions — and have been heavily abused in syndicated deals.

Cooperatives. Much of US agriculture is owned by farmers collectively: CHS, Land O'Lakes, Dairy Farmers of America, Growmark, Ag Processing Inc and hundreds of local elevator co-ops. Co-ops return profits to members as patronage dividends, which receive their own tax treatment, and they give farmers a share of the margins further down the chain.
12 / From farm to plate

Where the food dollar goes

Commodity prices are a small part of what consumers pay for food. Most of the retail price is processing, packaging, transport, labour, energy and retail margin.

USDA's food dollar series puts the farm share of what Americans spend on food at roughly fifteen cents in the dollar, with the rest going to processing, packaging, transport, retail, food service, energy and finance. For a box of cereal, the corn or wheat inside is worth a few cents. For fresh produce, eggs and meat, the farm share is far higher, which is why those are the products whose retail prices move with farm prices.

Price transmission is asymmetric and slow. When farm prices rise sharply, retail prices usually follow within months; when farm prices fall, retail prices fall more slowly, if at all. The pattern is clearest in meat, where the spread between what packers pay for cattle and what they receive for boxed beef widened dramatically in 2020–21, prompting antitrust investigations and lawsuits against the four largest packers, several of which were settled.

Protein

Cattle cycle

Cattle take nearly three years from conception to slaughter. Drought and high feed costs shrank the US herd to its smallest in decades by 2025, and cattle and retail beef prices rose to records. Repeated suspensions of Mexican feeder cattle imports after New World screwworm detections from 2025 tightened supply further.

Protein

Eggs and avian flu

Highly pathogenic avian influenza, which requires whole flocks to be culled on detection, sent US egg prices to records in early 2025. Egg prices are among the most volatile in the grocery store because laying flocks cannot be rebuilt quickly.

Retail

Concentration downstream

Grocery retail is concentrated — Walmart alone is the largest US grocer — as are packaged-food manufacturers. Retailers set shelf prices with a view to traffic and competition, not to farm costs.

Waste

Loss and waste

A large share of food produced is lost between field and fork. The economics of waste — cosmetic standards, cold-chain failures, date labels — matter as much to food prices as yield.

13 / Tricks of the trade

The promotional playbook

Agricultural markets attract promoters because they combine real volatility, compelling narratives about feeding the world, and illiquid assets that are hard to value.

Assets

"Farmland is uncorrelated"

Farmland returns look smooth because they are appraised, not traded. Appraisal smoothing hides volatility; the 1980s showed what happens when values are tested by forced sales. Look at transaction-based data and at leverage.

Assets

Agtech at peak valuation

Vertical farms, indoor growers and plant-based meat companies raised billions on extrapolated growth; several high-profile indoor farming companies went bankrupt in 2023–24. Ask what the unit economics are per pound against field-grown product, including energy.

Markets

Commodity index returns

Long-only commodity indices earn spot price changes plus or minus roll yield, plus collateral interest. In a market in persistent carry, rolling a long position loses money every month even if spot prices rise. Investors repeatedly mistake spot charts for investable returns.

Markets

Weather-market promotions

Every summer, newsletters and brokers pitch options on drought. Implied volatility is priced for exactly that fear, and most summer weather rallies fade. The premium is the product being sold.

Data

"Record exports" and flash sales

Export sale announcements can be cancelled, rolled or switched to "unknown destinations". Compare sales with actual inspections and shipments, and political purchase pledges with what crosses the water.

Data

Crop tour yields

Private crop tours sample a few thousand fields and are widely reported. They are useful indicators and imperfect forecasts; the final USDA number has diverged from tour estimates by large margins.

Physical

Warehouse receipt fraud

The 1963 salad oil scandal — Tino De Angelis borrowing against tanks of soybean oil that were mostly seawater — bankrupted brokers and shook Wall Street. Similar frauds, from Chinese metals warehouses to grain elevators, recur whenever lenders rely on paper rather than inspection.

Physical

Elevator insolvency

When a grain elevator fails, farmers holding unpaid delivered grain or deferred payment contracts can lose their crop. State indemnity funds cover some losses. Know whether your buyer is licensed and bonded, and avoid deferred-payment exposure to a weak counterparty.

Policy

RIN and biofuel credit fraud

Several fraud cases have involved RINs generated for biodiesel that was never made. Biofuel economics depend on credits whose value is set by regulation, which is a rich environment for fabrication.

Claims

Soil carbon credits

Paying farmers to store carbon in soil is promising and hard to verify: measurement is expensive, storage can reverse with a single tillage pass, and additionality is often unclear. Read the permanence and measurement terms before the price.

Claims

"Regenerative" premiums

Claims that a practice change brings both higher yields and premium prices deserve evidence by field and year. Some programmes deliver; some are marketing. Ask who pays the premium and for how long.

Insurance

Planting for the policy

Insurance designs can make it rational to plant marginal ground or a crop the farmer expects to fail, because the guarantee exceeds the market return. Regulators police this through yield history rules; it still shapes acreage at the margin.

Two questions that do most of the work

What are stocks-to-use, and where is the market on the curve? A bullish story in a market with ample stocks is a story; the same story with tight stocks is a price move.

Is this a price you can actually get? A futures quote, a spot index chart or a farmland appraisal is not the cash price at your elevator, the return on a rolled position, or the price a buyer will pay in a forced sale. Find the investable number.

15 / The present moment

Where agriculture stands in October 2026

Big crops, a fertiliser shock from the Gulf, soybeans propped up by China and biofuels, and margins near breakeven for corn.

Data as of 15 October 2026. Figures and events in this section were current at the v1.0 build and were not re-checked for v1.1; treat it as a dated snapshot. Claim-by-claim verification against primary sources is scheduled for the next major version.

1. A fertiliser shock that the crop price did not match

The effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz from 28 February removed a large share of the world's traded nitrogen fertiliser — AMIS estimated about 2.6 million tonnes of urea exports over two months — just as the northern hemisphere began spring application. Urea roughly doubled, from around $400 a tonne to over $850 in April, before easing to the mid-$400s by June after China lifted its urea export ban with a price floor and spring demand passed. The World Bank still projects urea averaging nearly 60% higher in 2026 than 2025. The farmer's problem is the gap: food commodity prices barely rose while input costs surged, an affordability squeeze that the FAO, IFPRI and the WTO have all flagged, with some farmers trimming nitrogen and shifting to less fertiliser-intensive crops.

2. Big US crops, weaker corn

USDA's 9 October WASDE surprised the market by raising the US corn crop 234 million bushels to 16.0 billion, lifting projected ending stocks by 282 million to about 1.85 billion and cutting the season-average price to $4.70. December corn fell 30 cents in half an hour, then recovered to around $4.83 by 12 October. Soybeans were projected at a record 53.1 bushels an acre and 4.56 billion bushels, with ending stocks of 315 million and a $12.00 season-average price unchanged. Harvest was running behind the five-year average in early October. At these prices, many Corn Belt operations on rented ground are near or below full-cost breakeven for corn, while soybeans are comfortably profitable.

3. Soybeans: China and the crush

Soybean futures near $12.90 are well above where they traded a year ago, for two reasons. China resumed buying under the November 2025 Busan arrangement — 25 million tonnes a year through 2028, met so far through state purchasers while its 10% retaliatory tariff stays in place — and was reported after a later Beijing summit to have pledged around $17 billion a year of US farm purchases. And biofuel policy has kept soybean oil above 65 cents a pound, supporting the crush. Both supports are political, which means both can change by announcement; the Supreme Court's February 2026 IEEPA ruling also raised questions about the tariff architecture on which the China arrangements were negotiated.

4. Wheat and livestock

Wheat stocks are tighter than last year — USDA projects 740 million bushels, about 20% lower — but US hard red winter wheat has struggled to compete for exports at prices well above a year ago. In livestock, the multi-year contraction in the cattle herd has kept cattle prices historically high, and herd rebuilding is slow.

1

Hormuz and nitrogen for 2027

Fall and spring fertiliser purchases for the 2027 crop depend on whether Gulf exports recover; the World Bank expects urea to ease about a quarter in 2027 if they do.

2

Corn acreage in 2027

With corn near breakeven and soybeans profitable, the soybean–corn price ratio this winter will shape next spring's acreage split.

3

China's shipments

Compare announced purchases with weekly export inspections, especially as the Brazilian crop arrives from January.

4

Washington

Further bridge payments, EPA biofuel volumes and refinery exemptions, and the farm bill debate each move farm income by billions.

Read every price with its date and place attached. December corn moved 30 cents in thirty minutes on one report day. A futures price is not a cash price, a cash price at one elevator is not the price at the next, and a USDA season-average forecast is an estimate that will be revised eleven times before it is final.
16 / Vocabulary

The words, defined

Filter by category or search. Grain-trade vocabulary is old, compact and precise — most of it was settled on the floor in Chicago before anyone alive was born.

BushelCrops

A volume measure converted to standard weights: 56 lb for corn, 60 lb for soybeans and wheat.

No. 2 yellow cornCrops

The standard grade deliverable against CBOT corn futures. Grading makes grain fungible.

Hard red winter / soft red winterCrops

Wheat classes: HRW (Kansas, bread flour) trades in Kansas City; SRW (eastern Corn Belt, crackers and cakes) in Chicago.

Hard red spring / durumCrops

High-protein northern wheat for premium bread flour, and the hard wheat used for pasta.

SafrinhaCrops

Brazil's second-season corn crop, planted after soybeans. Now larger than its first crop.

Crop rotationCrops

Alternating corn and soybeans on the same field; soybeans leave nitrogen for the following corn crop.

Trend yieldCrops

Long-run yield trend — about two bushels per acre per year for US corn. The baseline every season is measured against.

Feed conversionCrops

Pounds of feed per pound of meat: roughly 2 for chicken, 3–4 for pork, 6+ for feedlot beef.

Distillers grains (DDGS)Crops

The protein-rich feed co-product of corn ethanol production.

CrushCrops

Processing soybeans into meal and oil. One bushel yields about 11 lb of oil and 44–48 lb of meal.

Cash rentFarm & inputs

A fixed per-acre payment from tenant to landowner. The tenant bears all price and yield risk.

Crop shareFarm & inputs

A lease in which landlord and tenant split the crop and some costs.

Breakeven priceFarm & inputs

The price per bushel at which revenue covers all costs at a given yield.

Corn–urea ratioFarm & inputs

Bushels of corn needed to buy a ton of urea. A measure of fertiliser affordability.

Haber-BoschFarm & inputs

The process that makes ammonia from natural gas and air. The basis of almost all nitrogen fertiliser.

Urea / UAN / anhydrousFarm & inputs

The main nitrogen fertiliser forms: solid urea (46% N), liquid UAN solution, and anhydrous ammonia (82% N).

NPKFarm & inputs

Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium: the three main fertiliser nutrients.

Biotech traitFarm & inputs

A licensed genetic modification in seed — insect resistance, herbicide tolerance.

Precision agricultureFarm & inputs

GPS guidance, variable-rate application and yield mapping to manage fields metre by metre.

Tile drainageFarm & inputs

Buried drainage pipe that removes excess water, making poorly drained soils highly productive.

WASDESupply & demand

USDA's monthly World Agricultural Supply and Demand Estimates: the central balance-sheet report.

Balance sheetSupply & demand

Beginning stocks + production + imports − use = ending stocks, for a crop and year.

Ending stocksSupply & demand

Grain left at the end of the marketing year. The market's measure of scarcity.

Stocks-to-useSupply & demand

Ending stocks as a share of annual use. Price responds non-linearly: tight ratios mean violent prices.

Marketing yearSupply & demand

The crop accounting year: September–August for US corn and soybeans, June–May for wheat.

Prospective PlantingsSupply & demand

USDA's 31 March survey of farmers' planting intentions.

Acreage reportSupply & demand

USDA's 30 June survey of actual planted acres, released with quarterly Grain Stocks.

Crop ProgressSupply & demand

USDA's weekly report on planting, condition and harvest progress.

Weather marketSupply & demand

The summer period when prices trade on forecasts for pollination and pod-fill weather.

Export sales / inspectionsSupply & demand

USDA's weekly data on sales contracted to foreign buyers and on grain actually inspected for export.

Country elevatorLogistics & basis

A local facility that buys, grades, dries, stores and ships farmers' grain.

Shuttle trainLogistics & basis

A dedicated train of 100+ grain cars running repeatedly between a high-capacity loader and an export terminal.

Barge freightLogistics & basis

The cost of moving grain on the Illinois and Mississippi rivers. Spikes when water is low.

Export elevatorLogistics & basis

A terminal that loads ocean vessels. The Gulf and Pacific Northwest dominate US exports.

BasisLogistics & basis

Local cash price minus nearby futures. Reflects transport, local supply and demand, and storage.

CarryLogistics & basis

The premium of deferred futures over nearby. Pays for storage when close to full carrying cost.

InverseLogistics & basis

Nearby futures above deferred. The market wants grain now; storage loses money.

ConvergenceLogistics & basis

The coming together of futures and cash prices at delivery points as a contract expires.

Warehouse receiptLogistics & basis

A document of title to stored grain; collateral for loans and the basis of some futures delivery.

ABCDTrade & processing

ADM, Bunge, Cargill and Louis Dreyfus: the four historic giants of global grain trading.

OriginationTrade & processing

Buying grain from farmers. Trading houses compete on origination networks.

Board crushTrade & processing

The futures spread of soybean meal and oil against soybeans: the market's crush margin.

Renewable Fuel StandardTrade & processing

The US mandate requiring renewable fuels to be blended into transport fuel.

RINTrade & processing

Renewable Identification Number: a tradeable credit generated by biofuel production, used to demonstrate RFS compliance.

Renewable dieselTrade & processing

A diesel substitute made from fats and vegetable oils. Its growth reshaped soybean oil demand.

Packer spreadTrade & processing

The gap between what meatpackers pay for livestock and receive for meat.

Futures contractFutures

A standardised exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell a set quantity and grade for a future month at a price fixed today.

Clearing houseFutures

The exchange entity that becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, guaranteeing performance.

Initial marginFutures

The performance bond posted to open a futures position.

Mark to marketFutures

Daily settlement of gains and losses in cash. The source of margin calls.

Margin callFutures

A demand for more cash when losses deplete margin. The hidden cost of hedging in a rising market.

DeliveryFutures

Settling a futures contract by transferring the physical commodity or a shipping certificate.

Shipping certificateFutures

The delivery instrument for CBOT corn and soybeans: a warehouse's obligation to load grain at a river location.

Contract month codesFutures

F Jan, G Feb, H Mar, J Apr, K May, M Jun, N Jul, Q Aug, U Sep, V Oct, X Nov, Z Dec.

TickFutures

The minimum price move: a quarter-cent per bushel on CBOT grains, worth $12.50 per contract.

Price limitFutures

The maximum daily price move allowed in a futures contract, reset periodically.

Open interestFutures

The number of futures contracts outstanding. Rising open interest with rising price suggests new buying.

Commitments of TradersFutures

The CFTC's weekly breakdown of futures positions by type of trader.

Managed moneyFutures

Hedge funds and commodity trading advisers. Their positioning amplifies moves.

Roll yieldFutures

The gain or loss from replacing an expiring futures contract with a later one. Negative in a carry market for a long position.

Put optionFutures

The right to sell futures at a strike price. A price floor for a producer, at the cost of a premium.

Call optionFutures

The right to buy futures at a strike price. A price cap for a buyer; sold by producers to fund puts.

Implied volatilityFutures

The volatility priced into options. In grains it rises into summer and collapses after.

HedgeHedging

A position that offsets the price risk of a physical holding or commitment.

Short hedgeHedging

Selling futures against grain owned or expected to be grown.

Long hedgeHedging

Buying futures to fix the cost of grain a processor or exporter will need.

Basis riskHedging

The risk that local cash prices move differently from the futures used to hedge.

Forward contractHedging

An agreement with a buyer, usually the elevator, to deliver a quantity at a fixed price and time.

Hedge-to-arriveHedging

A contract fixing the futures portion of the price, with basis set later.

Basis contractHedging

A contract fixing the basis, with the futures portion set later.

Minimum price contractHedging

An elevator contract that combines a cash sale with a call option, giving a floor and some upside.

Fence / collarHedging

Buying a put and selling a call to bound the price within a range at low or zero premium.

Natural hedgeHedging

The tendency of regional crop failures to raise prices, partly offsetting lost bushels.

The dominant US crop insurance policy, guaranteeing revenue based on yield history and futures prices.

The share of expected revenue or yield insured, typically 70–85%.

Agriculture Risk Coverage: pays when county revenue falls below a benchmark.

Emergency aid outside the farm bill, such as trade-war and bridge payments.

Multi-year US legislation setting commodity, conservation, nutrition and insurance programmes.

17 / What to retain

Twelve things worth keeping

If everything else evaporates, these survive.

1

One run a year

Farm supply responds to price with a season's lag, and decisions made in spring cannot be undone.

2

Revenue is price times yield

Both are outside the farmer's control, and the margin between them and costs is thin.

3

Nitrogen is natural gas

Fertiliser prices follow gas and gas geography. A war in the Gulf is a cost shock in Iowa.

4

Stocks-to-use sets price

And non-linearly: ample stocks mean quiet prices; tight stocks mean violent ones.

5

Cash = futures + basis

Every farm price is a Chicago price plus a local adjustment. Hedging removes the first and keeps the second.

6

Futures exist for farmers

Standardised grades, clearing and margin let price risk move from those who hold grain to those willing to bear it.

7

Margin calls are the hidden cost

A short hedge pays out cash when prices rise. Liquidity, not price, breaks grain businesses.

8

Don't over-hedge the crop you may not grow

Drought brings low yields and high prices together. Size hedges to insured bushels.

9

Insurance runs on futures

Revenue Protection is priced off February and October futures averages, and is designed to work with hedging.

10

The waist of the hourglass takes the margin

Traders, processors, packers and input suppliers are few; farmers and eaters are many.

11

Policy is demand

Biofuel mandates, purchase pledges and payments are as real as feed and food demand — and change by announcement.

12

Land is the balance sheet

Profits capitalise into land values, and land values are what farm crises destroy.

The question to ask first, always

What is projected stocks-to-use, and how does today's futures price compare with the full-cost breakeven on rented ground? Those two numbers tell you whether a price is cheap or expensive in the market's terms and whether farmers can live with it — which is to say, where acreage, policy and prices are headed next.
